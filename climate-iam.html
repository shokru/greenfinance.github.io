Climate–economy model

This interactive page explores how an integrated assessment model (IAM) links economic activity to climate outcomes. Follow emissions, atmospheric CO₂, temperature, damages, investment, and consumption. Then change the assumptions and compare two pathways.

A model to inspect, not a forecast

The equations and default values are illustrative. They show mechanisms and do not produce a calibrated forecast or a policy recommendation.

Model sequence: Output → CO₂ emissions → atmospheric concentration → temperature → damages → investment and consumption.

Equations and key concepts

The model runs in one-year steps from 2025 to 2100. It keeps the feedback loops visible while avoiding a large codebase or external data dependency.

Production and emissions

Potential output comes from capital, labour, and productivity. Emissions depend on output, carbon intensity, and the abatement choice.

Yt = AtKtαLt1−α
Et = σtYt(1 − μt)

Productivity grows at the selected rate. Carbon intensity declines at its selected rate. In the policy path, abatement rises smoothly toward its chosen maximum after the start year.

Carbon and temperature

Emissions add to an atmospheric carbon stock. Carbon exchanges with a slower ocean reservoir. CO₂ concentration changes radiative forcing, which warms the atmosphere and ocean layers at different speeds.

Ft = 5.35 ln(Ct / C₀)
ΔTa ∝ Ft − λTa − γ(Ta − To)

Here C₀ = 278 ppm. The equilibrium feedback λ is set from climate sensitivity. A second temperature state represents slow heat uptake by the deep ocean.

Damages, costs, and investment

Temperature damages and abatement costs reduce output available to the economy. A fixed share of net output is invested; the rest is consumed.

D(T) = ψT² / (1 + ψT²)
Qt = Yt(1 − D(Tt))(1 − θμt2.8)
Ct = (1 − s)Qt

Capital then accumulates from investment and depreciates. The displayed discounted consumption index discounts total consumption at the selected rate. It is a comparison metric, not an optimized social welfare solution or a social cost of carbon.

Why compare two scenarios?

Holding the assumptions fixed isolates the role of the policy path. The gap between lines is the model’s implied difference under these equations, not a causal estimate from observed data.

Δ outcome = outcomemitigation − outcomeno additional mitigation

Mitigation has an immediate modeled cost and a delayed climate benefit. The balance depends on the parameters, timing, and the model’s simplified structure.

Key terms

Integrated assessment model (IAM)
A model that combines economic activity with environmental or climate processes to study how they affect one another.
Abatement, μ
The share of emissions avoided through mitigation. In this model, μ = 0 means no additional mitigation; μ = 0.8 means an 80% reduction in emissions relative to the technology path.
Carbon intensity, σ
CO₂ emitted per unit of economic output. Falling intensity represents cleaner energy, efficiency gains, or both.
Radiative forcing
A change in Earth’s energy balance. The model approximates the CO₂ contribution as 5.35 × ln(C / C₀), in watts per square metre.
Climate sensitivity
The eventual global temperature response to a doubling of atmospheric CO₂, measured in °C. The model uses a two-layer heat response, so warming takes time.
Damage function
A simplified mapping from global mean temperature to lost output. Its quadratic form captures rising damages, but cannot represent local exposure or tipping points.

Scope and further reading

The model is useful for reasoning about mechanisms. It leaves out many features that matter for scientific assessment and policy design.

What is simplified

  • One global economy and one global temperature. No countries, sectors, households, or distributional effects.
  • CO₂ is the only greenhouse gas. The model omits methane, nitrous oxide, aerosols, land-use change, and natural variability.
  • Climate damages are smooth and quadratic. No explicit extremes, adaptation, biodiversity loss, tipping points, or sea-level rise.
  • Mitigation is a chosen pathway, not the result of an optimization problem. The model does not calculate a defensible social cost of carbon.

How to use it in class

  • Change one parameter and explain the channel before interpreting the chart.
  • Compare early with delayed mitigation. Separate the near-term cost from the long-run temperature effect.
  • Raise climate sensitivity and the damage coefficient separately. They enter different parts of the model.
  • Use outputs as a controlled illustration. Do not present them as current NGFS or IPCC scenario estimates.
Calibration note: defaults are chosen for clear classroom behavior. A quantitative application would require calibration, validation against data, uncertainty analysis, and a documented version of the full model.
